Screen setup

1. Match a bank card to size the letters

Hold any credit, debit or ID card flat against the screen inside the outline. Drag the slider until the outline matches the card edges exactly.

card

2. Viewing distance

The clinic uses 36 inches. Any distance works because letter size scales to keep the same visual angle, so pick one you can repeat every time.

3. Room and screen

  • Turn off color filters: Night Shift, True Tone, Night Light, f.lux, blue-light or dark-reader extensions. They shift the colors this page computes.
  • Dark room. The clinic test is done with room lights off. Give your eyes a minute to settle.
  • Same brightness every time, around the middle. Changing it between runs makes scores harder to compare.
  • Cover one eye with your palm without pressing on it. Wear your usual distance glasses or contacts, never tinted or color-correcting lenses.
  • Answer fast. Each letter is gone in 1 to 1.6 seconds. If you are not sure, guess. A guess is right 1 time in 10 and a blank answer never is.

How the clinic test works

  • Each eye is tested on its own, in a dark room, at 36 inches.
  • You see 20 red letters (L-cone), then 20 green (M-cone), then 20 blue (S-cone). Each letter sits inside a crosshair on a gray screen.
  • The letters fade in 10 steps of 0.16 log units, two letters per step. Red and green go from 27.5% down to 1% cone contrast. Blue goes from 173% down to about 6–7%.
  • Each letter shows for 1.0 to 1.6 seconds, longer as it gets fainter, followed by blank gray for the same time.
  • Score per color is letters correct × 5, so 0 to 100. 75 or more is normal. The FAA accepts the military version at 55 or more in each color.

What practice can and cannot change

Practice cannot change how the cones respond. On a properly run clinic test, the score mostly reflects actual color vision. What practice can fix are avoidable misses: hesitating past the short window, not guessing, losing focus on the fainter letters, or being surprised by the format. A practice score here can differ from a clinic score by a few letters, because home screens are not calibrated.

The FAA does not accept color-correcting lenses such as X-Chrom or EnChroma for testing.

Sources Rabin, Gooch & Ivan (2011). Rapid quantification of color vision: the cone contrast test. Invest Ophthalmol Vis Sci 52:816–820. FAA Guide for Aviation Medical Examiners, Item 52: acceptable color vision tests. AOPA: Types of color vision tests.
